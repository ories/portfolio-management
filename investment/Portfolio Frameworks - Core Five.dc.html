BCG Growth–Share · In detail

Where Growth–Share came from, and how to build one

Bruce Henderson, Boston Consulting Group, 1970.

Where it came from

Cash is the scarce resource

Henderson built the matrix on the experience curve: cumulative production volume lowers unit cost, so market share converts into margin. A portfolio should therefore be self-funding. The products with cost advantage in slow markets pay for the products fighting for position in fast ones.

The unit of analysis is a revenue-generating product in a defined segment, and the currency is cash rather than revenue or headcount.

How to run it

Five steps

01Define the segment in writing, including who the largest competitor is.
02Relative share = your revenue ÷ largest competitor's revenue in that segment.
03Segment growth = YoY growth of the segment, not of your product.
04Plot each product, bubble size = ARR, so scale is visible.
05Apply the cutoffs (10% growth, 1.0× share) and read the quadrant.
How to read it

Watch the direction of cash

The output is a direction of cash: cows fund stars and a small number of question marks; question marks are options with an expiry date; dogs release capacity rather than earn it.

The software caveat: share buys cost advantage through distribution, data and platform reuse rather than manufacturing volume. Net revenue retention and gross margin are the better local proxies for "cow", and a product with 0.3× share can still be structurally advantaged if it owns the workflow.

BCG Growth–Share · Where it breaks

Three ways this matrix lies to you

01
Market share is a guess

In software, segment boundaries are drawn by whoever wants the budget. Define the segment before you measure share, and write the definition down.

02
It ignores what products share

A dog that carries your identity layer, your data model or your enterprise contracts is not a dog. Score dependencies separately.

03
Blind to option value

The matrix has no opinion on option value. Early bets that could reset the category always look like question marks worth killing.

Use it when

You have four or more revenue-generating products and a capacity fight to settle.

Don't

Single-product companies, pre-revenue bets, or anything you'd defend on strategic-option grounds.

Innovation Ambition · In detail

What the ambition split is actually claiming

Bansi Nagji and Geoff Tuff, Harvard Business Review, 2012.

Where it came from

Ambition levels need different rules

Innovation fails as a budget line because core work and distant bets compete for the same money under the same metrics, and the core always wins, because its returns are nearer and better evidenced.

Nagji and Tuff's fix is to classify every investment by its distance from today's products and today's customers, then govern each band differently: different funding gates, different metrics, different tolerance for failure.

How to run it

Five steps

01List every funded investment, including the ones nobody calls innovation.
02Classify each on both axes: how new is the customer, how new is the product or asset.
03Sum actual spend per band. Engineers, not intentions.
04Compare the real split against your stated ratio.
05Set metrics per band and a gate per band, then ring-fence the teams.
How to read it

The gap is the finding

The output is an allocation and, more usefully, the distance between the allocation you announced and the one your timesheets describe. Most portfolios discover they are running 90/10/0.

The 2012 study found 70/20/10 among outperformers, with a roughly inverted return profile. Treat it as a benchmark. Technology firms in that same study sat nearer 45/40/15. Mature category under attack: push transformational up. Fast-growing core with real retention: 80/15/5 is defensible.

Rules that make the split real
Separate the funding gates

Core is annual and headcount-based. Transformational is milestone-based and can be stopped mid-quarter.

Different metrics per band

Core is judged on NRR and margin. Transformational is judged on learning velocity and evidence, not revenue.

Shift the ratio deliberately

Mature category under attack: push to 50/30/20. Fast-growing core: 80/15/5 is defensible. Name the number and the reason.

Innovation Ambition · Where it breaks

Governance kills the transformational band

01
The split becomes a slogan

Announced in January, quietly 95/5/0 by March as core incidents pull people back. Ring-fence the teams or drop the claim.

02
Relabelling instead of reallocating

Core roadmap items get renamed "transformational" to look ambitious. Test it: would this ship to a customer you have today?

03
Core work looks unglamorous

The core band is where the money is. Staff it with your strongest people, or the funding for everything else disappears.

Use it when

Setting an annual budget, or explaining why a bet gets funded on different terms.

Don't

Use it to rank individual features. It works on portfolios of investment, not backlogs.

Ansoff Growth Matrix · In detail

The oldest of the five, and the simplest to abuse

Igor Ansoff, “Strategies for Diversification”, Harvard Business Review, 1957.

Where it came from

Growth has only four directions

Ansoff's argument was that any growth initiative is some combination of product novelty and market novelty, and that each kind of novelty introduces its own class of unknown: a technical one, a demand one, or both at once.

The value is in naming which unknowns you have taken on, so two initiatives asking for the same money can be compared on more than their forecast.

How to run it

Five steps

01State the initiative as one sentence: sell X to Y.
02Classify X and Y as existing or new using the buyer test. A different buyer or a different budget line means new.
03Assign the risk multiplier: penetration 1×, product development 2×, market development 4×, diversification 8×.
04Require the expected return to clear that multiplier, not a flat hurdle rate.
05Sequence the cheapest unknowns first, and hold the 8× move as an option.
How to read it

Use it to set the bar

Used well, Ansoff is a sequencing tool: it forces penetration work to be exhausted before new products are funded, and it prices diversification honestly against the alternatives.

The multipliers are heuristics, widely quoted and not empirically derived. Their job is discipline. A diversification bet has to clear a visibly higher bar than a pricing project of the same size. Treat them as a ratio between options, never as an expected loss.

Ansoff Growth Matrix · Where it breaks

It names the move. It will not make the call

01
"New" is doing a lot of work

A new tier for existing buyers is not a new product. Define new as a different buyer or a different budget line.

02
Nothing about capacity

The matrix will happily suggest four moves you cannot staff. Pair every quadrant with a named team, or it is a wish list.

03
Penetration gets skipped

It is the least exciting box and usually the highest return. Most portfolios have a pricing or activation project worth more than their next new product.

Use it when

Sequencing growth initiatives and pricing their risk against each other.

Don't

Treat it as a portfolio review. It says nothing about what to stop doing.

Wardley Mapping · In detail

Position, movement, and what to stop building

Simon Wardley, Creative Commons, from 2005.

Where it came from

Everything drifts toward commodity

A map starts from a user need and chains downward through every component that need depends on. Each component is then placed on an evolution axis by its characteristics: how uncertain, how differentiating, how widely available.

The claim is that supply and demand competition pushes every component rightward over time, and that the correct practice for a component depends entirely on where it currently sits, rather than on what it was when you first built it.

How to run it

Five steps

01Write the user need at the top. Not a feature, a need.
02Chain the components beneath it until you reach things you rent.
03Place each component on the evolution axis: genesis, custom-built, product, or commodity.
04Mark the direction of travel, and mark where you have inertia: sunk cost, a team, a contract.
05Apply doctrine per stage: build genesis, build custom where you differentiate, buy product, rent commodity.
How to read it

The uncomfortable column

Two errors show up on almost every map. Custom-building something that is already a commodity, which is invisible cost. And buying something in genesis that is your actual differentiator, which is outsourced strategy.

The output is shared situational awareness rather than a priority list, and the argument about placement is where most of the value sits. Redraw it when the market moves, not on a schedule.

Wardley Mapping · Applied

What the map told Arclight to stop doing

Decision 01
Kill the in-house identity service

Nine years old, four engineers, zero differentiation. It sits in the commodity column and is bought by everyone else in the category. Migrate to a provider and redeploy the team to Flow.

Inertia: it was someone's promotion.

Decision 02
Stop shopping for a workflow engine

Orchestration is the one custom-built component that customers choose Arclight for. Buying it would hand the differentiator to a vendor who also sells to competitors.

This reverses a procurement decision already in flight.

Decision 03
Rent model serving, own the reasoning layer

Inference is commoditising and its price is falling; the domain reasoning on top of it is genesis and nobody sells it. Build one, rent the other, and expect to renegotiate annually.

Revisit when serving prices halve again.

None of the other frameworks in this deck would have surfaced the identity service. It has no revenue line, so it never appears as a product.

Wardley Mapping · Where it breaks

A map nobody redraws is a poster

01
Placement becomes the meeting

Teams spend two hours arguing whether something is product or commodity. Timebox it: the column matters, the exact position within it does not.

02
It needs a facilitator

Unlike a 2×2, a map is hard to produce well on a first attempt. Expect the first two to be wrong and treat them as practice.

03
It will not prioritise for you

A map tells you what to build, buy or rent. It will not rank your initiatives or size your bets, so pair it with something that does.

Use it when

You are deciding build versus buy, or you suspect you are custom-building commodities.

Don't

You need a funding split or a ranked backlog. The map has no opinion on either.

Cost of Delay and WSJF

Cost of Delay and WSJF

Donald Reinertsen, 2009. The only framework here that ranks rather than classifies.

The ratio
Weighted Shortest Job First = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Duration

Cost of Delay is what one week of waiting costs you. Duration is how long the work takes. Rank by the ratio, take from the top, re-rank next cycle.

Component 01
User and business value

Revenue protected or gained per week. The only component you can sometimes state in currency.

Component 02
Time criticality

Does the value decay? A renewal deadline, a competitor launch or a regulatory date all make waiting expensive.

Component 03
Risk and opportunity enablement

What this unblocks or de-risks. Where platform work earns its ranking instead of pleading for it.

Cost of Delay and WSJF · In detail

Queues are the cost nobody budgets

Donald Reinertsen, “The Principles of Product Development Flow”, 2009.

Where it came from

Time is the constraint

Without a cost of delay, an organisation optimises for what it can see: utilisation, headcount efficiency, cost per feature. Queues are invisible on every dashboard, so the economic cost of work sitting still never enters a decision.

Putting a number on a week of delay changes the argument from which initiative is most important to which order costs us least. That reordering is usually free.

How to run it

Five steps

01Estimate cost of delay per week for each initiative. In currency if your economics allow, otherwise on a relative 1-2-3-5-8-13 scale.
02Estimate duration, not effort. A six-week job with two people beats a two-week job that waits a quarter for a dependency.
03Divide to get the ratio, and sort.
04Take work from the top only, and resist splitting capacity across the whole list.
05Re-rank every cycle. The ranking is a snapshot of economics, and economics move.
How to read it

Short and valuable first

The counter-intuitive result is that a small, cheap, moderately valuable job frequently outranks a large, exciting, highly valuable one. That is the point of it.

Absolute scores mean nothing. Only the ordering does. And the ranking assumes you can actually start the top item. It says nothing about whether the right team is free, which is why it pairs with a capacity view rather than replacing one.

Cost of Delay and WSJF · Where it breaks

Numbers that only look economic

01
Relative scales get gamed

Once teams learn that 13s win, everything becomes a 13. Calibrate against two or three reference initiatives everyone already agrees on.

02
Duration is the weak input

People estimate effort and call it duration, ignoring the queue time that dominates. Measure real cycle time from finished work and use that instead.

03
A ranking is not a plan

WSJF assumes the top item can start now. Without a capacity view it produces an order nobody can execute, and the list quietly gets ignored.

Use it when

You have more initiatives than capacity and the argument has stalled on importance.

Don't

Use it to compare a discovery bet against a delivery project. Options and deliverables do not share a denominator.

In practice

Run it on a cadence, or don't run it

ANNUAL
Set the ambition split

Decide the core / adjacent / transformational ratio and ring-fence the teams.

SEMI-ANNUAL
Redraw the map

Wardley, from the current user need. Check what has drifted right since last time.

QUARTERLY
Check the cash map

Growth and share by product. Confirm the cows still fund the stars.

PER INITIATIVE
Name the growth move

Every funded initiative declares its Ansoff quadrant and clears that bar.

None of these five tell you what to do. They make disagreement specific, which is most of the work.
